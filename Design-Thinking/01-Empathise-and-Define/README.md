# Empathise and Define

## What This Stage Means

The first stage of Design Thinking begins with **people, not solutions**.

**Empathise** means understanding what people experience, feel, need, struggle with, and do in a real situation.

**Define** means using that understanding to describe the actual problem clearly.

These two parts belong together because a problem should not be defined only from assumptions.

```text
Observe + Listen + Ask
        |
        v
Understand the Person
        |
        v
Find the Real Need
        |
        v
Define the Problem
```

## Empathise

Students try to understand the situation from the user's point of view.

Useful methods include:

- observation,
- interviews,
- conversations,
- shadowing a process,
- journey mapping,
- empathy mapping,
- noting frustrations,
- noting workarounds people already use.

## What to Observe

Look for:

- what the person is trying to do,
- where the difficulty happens,
- what takes extra time,
- what causes confusion,
- what causes discomfort,
- when another person has to help,
- what the person avoids doing,
- what they say,
- what they actually do.

## Questions Students Can Ask

- What are you trying to do?
- Which part is difficult?
- When does the problem happen most often?
- What do you currently do when this happens?
- How does this affect your time, comfort, safety, or independence?
- Who else is affected?
- What would make the situation easier for you?

Avoid questions that push a solution, such as:

> Would you like an app that solves this?

That question already assumes the answer.

## Define

After collecting evidence, students organise what they learned.

A good problem definition identifies:

- the person or group,
- the situation,
- the difficulty,
- the need,
- the reason the problem matters.

## Problem Statement Format

A useful format is:

```text
[Person / user] experiences difficulty with [specific situation]
because [evidence-based reason or condition], which affects
[comfort / safety / time / access / independence / participation].
```

## Example

Too broad:

> Elderly people have mobility problems.

More useful:

> Older adults with limited knee movement find it difficult to enter a community building that can only be reached by a steep flight of steps, reducing their ability to use the service independently.

The second statement describes a person, context, difficulty, and impact without suggesting a solution.

## Student Evidence

Students should keep:

- observation notes,
- interview questions,
- important quotations or paraphrased comments,
- empathy map,
- list of assumptions,
- evidence that challenged an assumption,
- final problem statement.

## Check Before Moving Forward

Ask:

- Did we talk to or observe real users where appropriate?
- Are we describing the problem rather than a solution?
- Is the problem specific enough to investigate?
- Can we explain why it matters to the person?
- Which part is supported by evidence?
