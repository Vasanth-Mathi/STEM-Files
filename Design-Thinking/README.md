# Design Thinking

Design Thinking is a **human-centred and iterative approach to problem-solving**.

It begins by understanding people and their real experiences before deciding what should be built or changed.

Students do not begin with:

> What can I make?

They begin with:

> Who is experiencing a difficulty, what is happening, and why does it matter?

## The Design Thinking Cycle

For this repository, the cycle is organised into five connected stages:

```text
Define Empathy
        |
        v
      Ideate
        |
        v
    Prototype
        |
        v
     Evaluate
        |
        v
      Reflect
        |
        +--------------------+
        |                    |
        v                    |
Return to any earlier stage -+
when new evidence changes understanding
```

Design Thinking is not a rigid five-step recipe. Evaluation or reflection may show that the original problem was misunderstood, which means the team may need to return to empathy or redefine the problem.

## 1. Define Empathy

Understand the people, context, difficulties, needs, and emotions involved.

Use observation, interviews, conversations, and evidence.

Then define the problem clearly without hiding a solution inside the statement.

**Main question:**  
*What is the real problem from the user's point of view?*

## 2. Ideate

Generate several possible responses to the defined problem.

Delay judgement long enough to explore different directions.

**Main question:**  
*What different possibilities could respond to this need?*

## 3. Prototype

Create a simple representation that lets the team test an important assumption.

A prototype does not need to be finished or beautiful.

**Main question:**  
*What is the simplest thing we can make to learn something useful?*

## 4. Evaluate

Test the prototype using criteria, observation, measurements, and user feedback.

Do not ask only whether people "like" it.

**Main question:**  
*What actually happened when the idea was tested?*

## 5. Reflect

Examine what changed in the team's understanding.

Record successes, failures, limitations, evidence, and the next question.

**Main question:**  
*What did we learn, and what should change because of that learning?*

## What Makes a Good Design Thinking Problem

A useful Design Thinking problem is usually:

- experienced by identifiable people,
- specific enough to observe,
- connected to a real activity or context,
- important to the people affected,
- open enough that several responses are possible,
- not already written as a preferred solution.

Too broad:

> Traffic is a problem.

More useful:

> Older pedestrians find it difficult to cross a wide road before the pedestrian signal changes.

Already solution-shaped:

> We need an app for elderly pedestrians.

The second statement allows investigation. The third has already chosen a solution before empathy.

## Student Evidence Across the Cycle

A strong Design Thinking portfolio can include:

- observation notes,
- empathy map,
- defined problem statement,
- assumptions,
- idea sketches,
- decision criteria,
- prototype versions,
- testing plan,
- results and feedback,
- Version 1 / Version 2 comparison,
- limitations,
- individual reflection.
